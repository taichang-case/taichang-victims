How to report a fake investment platform — step by step

Written for people who deposited money into a platform that now refuses to pay out. Applies to any cloned broker site, “withdrawal tax” app, or pig-butchering platform. Last updated: 2026-11-25.

First, stop paying. Any request for a tax, unlock fee, verification fee or “credit score deposit” before releasing your own money is part of the scam. Nothing you pay will unlock the account. This page charges nothing and never asks for passwords or ID documents.

1. Stop paying, and do not accept “recovery” offers

Two things happen after a platform blocks your withdrawal. First, the operator demands a fee to release your balance. Second, other people contact you offering to recover the funds for an upfront payment — they target victims of the first scam. Neither path returns money. Real investigators never ask a victim to pay first.

2. Save these five things before anything disappears

  1. The platform URL and the app name exactly as you saw them, plus screenshots of the login page and the balance screen.
  2. Every deposit: date, amount, currency, and the address or bank account you sent to.
  3. The transaction hash (txid) of each deposit. In most wallets this is a 64-character string; on Ethereum-style chains it begins with 0x.
  4. The chat logs with the “account manager” or “customer service”, including the fee demands.
  5. The names, handles or phone numbers those people used.

The transaction hash matters more than any screenshot: it is the only thing that ties your loss to a wallet investigators can act on.

3. File with the FBI (IC3)

Go to ic3.gov and file a complaint. Choose the cryptocurrency or investment fraud category. It is free, takes roughly fifteen minutes, and returns a complaint ID that you should keep and quote in any later correspondence. If the platform is one of the domains seized in the Tai Chang case, you can also write to TaiChangTIPS@fbi.gov or call FBI San Diego at (858) 320-1800.

4. Tell your bank, quickly

Card payments and wire transfers have the shortest recovery windows. Call the bank, describe the transaction as fraud, and ask them to log it and attempt a recall. Do this in parallel with the IC3 filing — the bank does not wait for the FBI.

5. File locally as well

National and local police reports create a second record and, in some countries, are a precondition for later restitution. Keep the report number. For US victims the three channels — IC3, the case mailbox, and local police — are separate; doing all three costs nothing.

6. What not to send anyone

  • Passwords, 2FA codes, bank logins, or full ID documents.
  • Any further payment, including “legal fees” or “release fees”.
  • Remote-access software installed at someone’s request.
If the app is still installed: the installation file (APK) is useful evidence — these apps are distributed outside official stores, so investigators rarely get a sample. You can send the file to taichang-case@outlook.com. Never send passwords, codes or ID documents.

7. Why reporting still helps after the money is gone

Investigators work from wallets, not from complaints alone. A transaction hash lets them attach your loss to a wallet they are already tracking, and a complaint ID makes you a named victim when funds are eventually seized and forfeited. Reports also feed proactive programmes that identify victims before they lose more. Reporting is slow, but it is the only mechanism that leads to restitution.
